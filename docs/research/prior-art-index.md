# Prior-art index

Annotated index of projects and studies related to Custodian. Checked 2026-10-23 (Atlantic/Reykjavik); entries 9, 10 and 11 added 2026-10-24.

How to read each entry:
- **Confirmed**: checked against the primary source linked in the entry.
- **Self-description**: what a project says about itself. Not independently checked.
- **Not verified**: we could not find a primary source for it.

Status markers: **Verified** / **Partly verified** / **Not verified**.

Verification tiers follow [docs/graph-schema.md](../graph-schema.md). A tier describes the kind of source, not whether it is right.

## 1. Audrey Tang and Caroline Green: Civic AI and the 6-Pack of Care
**Status:** Verified (framework and authorship); the vTaiwan lineage is a statement the authors make.
**Verification tier (primary source):** institutional-report (Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI pages). civic.ai and its podcast: creator-content.

- **What they built or studied.** Confirmed: "Civic AI — 6-Pack of Care" is a research project by Audrey Tang and Caroline Green at the Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI (Accelerator Fellowship Programme). The six packs are Attentiveness, Responsibility, Competence, Responsiveness, Solidarity and Symbiosis. civic.ai describes the goal as AI "that the people it affects can check, correct and switch off". Packs 1–4 follow the four phases of care set out by Fisher and Tronto (1990). Self-description: the site says a book is due from Wiley in February 2027 and that the framework was presented at Rhodes House, Oxford, on 25 March 2026.
- **Lineage.** Self-description: the Oxford article says the framework builds "on real-world precedents such as the vTaiwan platform" and on the ⿻ Plurality vision.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** Pack 4 (Responsiveness): affected people can contest outcomes and force a repair. Custodian's challengeable flags and its "the system is not the editor" rule ask for the same thing.
- **Open questions.** Can the per-pack "public measure" serve as an audit check for Custodian's integrity layer? Pack 6 (one job, one place, time-limited) compared with Custodian's permanent record. Has the framework been tested against any deployed system?
- **Sources.** https://civic.ai/ · https://afp.oxford-aiethics.ox.ac.uk/civic-ai · https://afp.oxford-aiethics.ox.ac.uk/article/civic-ai-and-6-pack-care-reimagining-ai-alignment-relational-health · https://civic.ai/podcast/

## 2. Cornell team, Laude Institute Moonshot: "Rebuilding Trust in Civic Discourse: An Auditable AI-Mediated Infrastructure"
**Status:** Verified (grant and amounts, via the Laude page and Cornell Chronicle in the landscape brief).
**Verification tier (primary source):** institutional-report (Laude Institute page, Cornell Chronicle). The project is a proposal; no peer-reviewed output found.

- **What they built or studied.** Confirmed: the Cornell team (PI Jon Kleinberg, with Bazarova, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil, R. Kleinberg, Naaman and Rand) received a **$250,000 seed** from the Laude Institute and is competing for **$10M**. It is one of 8 seeds. The project is a *proposal*. It has three layers: AI assistants for individuals, AI moderators for groups, and an independent AI auditing layer that produces "a transparent record of each agent's interventions". It also has an adversarial track for stress-testing. No code was found.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** An audit trail as trust infrastructure. The difference: Cornell audits AI agents against their declared roles, while Custodian keeps a record of human speech and of the research it cites.
- **Open questions.** What exactly does the cryptographic verification cover? Would the adversarial track test Custodian's detectors? Also look at the second Laude civic team: Harvard/MIT "Deliberation at National Scale" (Procaccia et al., Frankly platform).
- **Sources.** https://www.laude.org/moonshots/one/rebuilding-trust-civic-discourse · https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/04/moonshot-project-aims-restore-trust-digital-public-sphere · https://www.laude.org/updates/moonshots-one

## 3. ZKorum / Agora Citizen Network
**Status:** Partly verified (licence, repo, funders and audit code confirmed; ZK identity and AT Protocol DDS are self-description).
**Verification tier (primary source):** creator-content (zkorum GitHub repos, agoracitizen.network). NGI TrustChain page: institutional-report.

- **What they built.** Confirmed: an open-source monorepo, `zkorum/agora`, licensed AGPL-3.0, maintained by ZKorum (Paris) and very active (HEAD `98edd28`, 2026-10-21). Funding comes from EU Horizon Europe through NGI TrustChain and NGI Sargasso. Self-description: zero-knowledge proofs of eligibility (for example ZK passport proofs), Polis-style opinion clustering and bridging ranking, and a planned move to the Decentralized Deliberation Standard (DDS) on AT Protocol with "tamper-evident verification".
- **Code findings (landscape brief, 2026-10-23).** From 2025-01 to 2026-02 the API published a proof of 8 write actions to a Nostr channel. **Moderation endpoints were never covered.** The watchdog witness never verified or stored proofs and has been dormant since 2025-03. The broadcast was removed in commit `d968353` (2026-02-22) because "the project is moving to DDS". Moderation today is ordinary mutable Postgres rows, and deletion is soft-delete followed by hard delete. Conclusion: there is no working verifiable-moderation feature to layer on.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** Proving eligibility without revealing identity, combined with preserved disagreement.
- **Open questions.** Will DDS cover moderation actions and deletions, and when? Has anyone audited the ZK passport route, and does it work in Iceland? The `zkorum/dds` and `zkorum/racine` repos have not been inspected.
- **Sources.** https://github.com/zkorum/agora · https://github.com/zkorum/agora/commit/d968353d5f1f34ae50f57a7392d078e251a3dc17 · https://github.com/zkorum/watchdog · https://trustchain.ngi.eu/zkorum/ · https://www.agoracitizen.network/

## 4. Pol.is / Computational Democracy Project
**Status:** Verified (licence, method, vTaiwan Uber case); funder not verified.
**Verification tier (primary source):** creator-content (pol.is, GitHub, pol.is blog). CrowdLaw case study: institutional-report.

- **What they built.** Confirmed: open-source software (AGPL-3.0, `compdemocracy/polis`). Participants agree, disagree or pass on short statements and cannot reply to each other directly. The software clusters opinion groups and shows consensus statements next to divisive ones.
- **vTaiwan Uber case (August 2015).** Confirmed: it was the first vTaiwan process to use Pol.is. There were 4 weeks of survey, 31,115 votes, 925 survey voters and 145 statements. A live-streamed two-hour meeting followed on 2015-08-27 with 1,875 online participants. Afterwards Uber committed to sharing its insurance policy and to having drivers registered (Audrey Tang's account on the pol.is blog; the CrowdLaw case study). Self-description (Co-Intelligence Institute): statements with more than 80% consensus set the agenda.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** The preserved-disagreement primitive in its working form.
- **Open questions.** Funder. Can Custodian take in Pol.is exports as an input to its discourse map? What AGPL obligations would apply?
- **Sources.** https://pol.is/ · https://github.com/compdemocracy/polis · https://compdemocracy.org/case-studies/2014-vtaiwan/ · https://blog.pol.is/uber-responds-to-vtaiwans-coherent-blended-volition-3e9b75102b9b · https://congress.crowd.law/files/vtaiwan-case-study.pdf

## 5. Bak-Coleman, West, O'Connor, Bergstrom (2026): "Industry Influence in High-Profile Social Media Research"
**Status:** Verified.
**Verification tier (primary source):** preprint (arXiv:2601.11507). No peer-reviewed version found.

- **What they studied.** Confirmed (arXiv:2601.11507): 295 social media articles in Science, Nature, PNAS and their transfer journals. 49% [43–55%] have disclosable ties to Meta, X, Google or Microsoft (funding, collaboration or employment), and fewer than half of those disclose them. 42% of papers that declared "no competing interests" still had ties. Industry-tied work receives roughly twice the attention. Ties are concentrated in few people (Gini 0.919). About 35% of papers with an academic editor were handled by an editor with ties. The estimated share of papers with industry involvement across production and review is 79.9%. The authors call their figures a conservative lower bound.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** It is a working example of incentive transparency: OpenAlex funding data joined to papers. That is the method Custodian's research mirror proposes.
- **Open questions.** The dataset is not released for de-identification reasons, so can the method be reproduced on open data alone? How well does it carry over to Icelandic or Nordic venues?
- **Sources.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11507

## 6. Briggs, Mellon, Arel-Bundock (2026): "It must be very hard to publish null results"
**Status:** Verified.
**Verification tier (primary source):** preprint (SocArXiv/OSF). I4R Discussion Paper: institutional-report.

- **What they studied.** Confirmed: 101,475 articles from 156 political science journals, 2010–2024, extracted with large language models and validated. Main text: "94% of abstracts explicitly report a non-null result". Only 2% report only a null result. A calibrated model estimates that significant results are "one to two orders of magnitude" (10–100×) more likely to enter the published record. Pre-registration and randomised experiments are associated with more nulls being accepted. The abstract itself says "over 90%". Preprint on SocArXiv/OSF (v2, 2026-05-08) and I4R Discussion Paper 281.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** Suppressed nulls are a kind of integrity gap the mirror is meant to show: what the published record leaves out.
- **Open questions.** The data will be released "upon article acceptance", so can it feed Custodian's mirror? Is there an equivalent for the fields Alþingi cites?
- **Sources.** https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/zr5vf_v2 · https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/zr5vf_v2 · https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/336819/1/I4R-DP281.pdf

## 9. South Africa: amnesty conditional on full disclosure (TRC, 1995 to 2001)
**Status:** Verified (mechanism, court reasoning, Committee's own account of the disclosure test). Partly verified (final tally: the only itemised table found is a 1 November 2000 snapshot). Checked 2026-10-24.
**Verification tier (primary sources):** institutional-report (the Act, the Constitutional Court judgment, the TRC Final Report Volume 6, the Department of Justice amnesty index). Gibson 2002: peer-reviewed-journal.

- **The mechanism.** Confirmed (Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act 34 of 1995, s 20(1)): the Amnesty Committee "shall grant amnesty" if the application complies with the Act, the act was "associated with a political objective committed in the course of the conflicts of the past" (criteria in s 20(2) and (3): motive, context, gravity, target, orders, proportionality; excluding personal gain, malice or spite), and "the applicant has made a full disclosure of all relevant facts". Effect (s 20(7)): no criminal or civil liability for the amnestied act, and none for the State or any organisation. Names of amnestied persons and enough detail to identify the act are published in the Gazette (s 20(6)). Convictions are expunged from official records (s 20(10)). Window: 1 March 1960 to a cut-off first set at 5 or 6 December 1993 and later extended to 10 May 1994 (TRC Vol 6 s 1, para 16 and fn 11).
- **Constitutional basis.** Confirmed: the 1993 interim Constitution's postamble, "National Unity and Reconciliation", states that "amnesty shall be granted in respect of acts, omissions and offences associated with political objectives and committed in the course of the conflicts of the past" and directs Parliament to set a cut-off date (quoted in AZAPO, below).
- **The court's incentive argument.** Confirmed (AZAPO and Others v President of the RSA, CCT 17/96, decided 25 July 1996, Mahomed DP, application dismissed; s 20(7) upheld). Para 17: the truth victims seek "is much more likely to be forthcoming if those responsible for such monstrous misdeeds are encouraged to disclose the whole truth with the incentive that they will not receive the punishment which they undoubtedly deserve if they do. Without that incentive there is nothing to encourage such persons to make the disclosures". Para 36 extends the same reasoning to civil liability: keeping civil claims alive would "discourage them from revealing that truth". This is the clearest primary-source statement of the bargain Custodian's book outline leans on: disclosure is bought with the waiver of punishment.
- **How "full disclosure" was read in practice.** Confirmed (TRC Final Report Vol 6, s 1, ch 1, paras 25 to 27): the Committee held that the duty "related solely to the particular incident" applied for, though it covered the applicant's own role and that of others, and later concealment. The Committee records the criticism that applicants "were able to hold back information" and that this "frustrated the very intention of the overall process", and accepts that the criticism "is serious and substantial". It answers that the limit came from the Act, not the Committee.
- **Scale.** Confirmed (Vol 6, s 1): "Out of a total of 7115 applications, 5489 were dealt with in chambers" (without a public hearing, generally for acts that were not gross human rights violations). First application 1 January 1996; more than 7,000 received by the extended closing date of 30 September 1997; Committee dissolved 31 May 2001 by Proclamation R31. Partly verified: the Department of Justice amnesty index gives, as of 1 November 2000, 849 granted and 5,392 refused out of 7,112, with 248 withdrawn and 302 decisions outstanding, and warns that its "result" field "should not be used for statistical purposes". The widely repeated 849 / 5,392 figures are this snapshot, not a final tally. No final itemised table was found in Vol 6 s 1.
- **Did people accept it?** Confirmed (Gibson 2002, AJPS 46(3) 540–556, doi:10.2307/3088398, survey plus vignette experiment): South Africans were broadly not opposed to amnesty but did not see it as fair in itself; perceived fairness rose when victims were compensated (distributive justice), given voice (procedural) and received apologies (restorative). Gibson warns that unmet expectations of justice could weaken the democratic transition. His 2004 book (Overcoming Apartheid, Russell Sage, survey of about 3,700) argues the truth process as a whole supported reconciliation; that broader claim is contested and is self-description here.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** The bargain is the thesis of docs/book-outline.md stated as law and upheld by a court: without a waiver of punishment, disclosure does not come. The TRC's own record also shows the limit: a narrow disclosure test let applicants disclose the minimum, and the public accepted amnesty only when paired with compensation, voice and apology. For Custodian this reads as: incentive transparency needs a low price for disclosing, and the price people will accept depends on what the record gives back to those harmed.
- **Open questions.** Is there a final Amnesty Committee tally in Vol 6 s 3 or in the 2003 handover? What proportion of grants rested on contested disclosure? Is there later empirical work (post-2004) on whether amnesty produced information that prosecutions would not have? How does the bargain translate from political crimes to undisclosed research ties, where the "punishment" is reputational and the "truth" is a funding relationship?
- **Sources.** https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za/act/1995/34 · http://www.acts.co.za/promotion-of-national-unity-and-reconciliation-act-1995/20__granting_of_amnesty_and_ef · https://reparations.qub.ac.uk/assets/uploads/AZAPO.pdf (judgment text) · https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/vol6_s1.pdf · https://sabctrc.saha.org.za/reports/volume6/section1/chapter1/subsection5.htm · https://www.justice.gov.za/trc/amntrans/index.htm · https://doi.org/10.2307/3088398 · https://www.russellsage.org/publications/overcoming-apartheid

## 10. Iceland: Þjóðfundur 2010, Stjórnlagaráð 2011 and the 2012 referendum
**Status:** Verified (statute, Supreme Court decision, Alþingi resolution and bill texts, Statistics Iceland referendum report). Partly verified (the 24 March 2011 vote tally; the law number of the 2013 temporary amendment provision). Checked 2026-10-24.
**Verification tier (primary sources):** institutional-report (Alþingi documents and Act texts, Hæstiréttur decision, stjórnlaganefnd report, Ministry of the Interior notice, Statistics Iceland). Landemore 2015: peer-reviewed-journal. Venice Commission opinion: institutional-report, existence and date confirmed, content not read.

- **Design.** Confirmed (Act 90/2010 on a Constitutional Assembly; stjórnlaganefnd report, vol 1, Þjóðfundur chapter): the Act called for an advisory constitutional assembly of 25 to 31 directly elected members and, as preparation, a national forum of about one thousand participants "valdir með slembiúrtaki úr þjóðskrá" (chosen by random sample from the national register), balanced by region and sex. The forum was held in Laugardalshöll on Saturday 6 November 2010. Attendance: "Fundinn sóttu 950 manns af landinu". Tables of eight with a facilitator; each table produced summary sentences. This is the statutory forum. The 2009 Þjóðfundur (civil-society, see research gaps) is a different event and remains unchecked.
- **The election and its annulment.** Confirmed (Hæstiréttur, decision of 25 January 2011, six justices): the 27 November 2010 election to the Assembly was declared void, "Kosning til stjórnlagaþings 27. nóvember 2010 er ógild." Defects listed: sequentially numbered ballots (traceable to voters), cardboard partitions that did not make a voting booth, ballots not folded, plastic ballot boxes that could not be locked, and counting not held in public with no candidate representatives present. Two justices dissented on the folding point. The Alþingi majority committee report (Þskj. 1028, 139th session) records that the court's own view was that the defects were of a kind that could affect the result, and that the decision "er endanleg og verður ekki vefengd".
- **The workaround.** Confirmed (Þingsályktun 19/139, Þskj. 1120, adopted 24 March 2011): Alþingi resolved to appoint a 25-member Stjórnlagaráð, offering seats to those the election board had allocated seats in the annulled election, with at least 10 seats per sex, and a mandate to take up the stjórnlaganefnd report and propose constitutional amendments. Partly verified: Alþingi documents describe the resolution as passed "naumlega" (narrowly); the exact tally was not found in a primary source. The Council adopted its bill unanimously on 27 July 2011 and delivered it to the Speaker on 29 July 2011 (Þskj. 831, 140th session).
- **The referendum.** Confirmed (Statistics Iceland, Hagskýrslur 2013:1; Ministry of the Interior notice, 30 October 2012): an advisory referendum ("Ráðgefandi þjóðaratkvæðagreiðsla") on 20 October 2012, ordered by an Alþingi resolution of 24 May 2012. Six questions. Electorate 236,850; votes cast 115,890; turnout 48.9%. Question 1, whether the Council's proposals should be the basis of a new constitution: 73,509 yes, 36,302 no, 66.9% yes of those answering. Lowest yes was the national church question (58,455). The ministry notice gives a slightly different roll (236,911) and invalid count (1,499 against the statistical report's 1,320); both are recorded here.
- **The handoff.** Confirmed: the constitutional bill (Þskj. 510, 415. mál, 141st session) was tabled by the majority of the Constitutional and Supervisory Committee on 16 November 2012 and had its first reading on 20 and 21 November 2012. The same committee chair asked the Venice Commission for an opinion on 16 November 2012; the opinion (CDL-AD(2013)010) was adopted 8 to 9 March 2013. The bill was not passed. In its place the 141st session passed a temporary amendment provision (Þskj. 1139, 641. mál): until 30 April 2017, a constitutional amendment could pass with a three-fifths Alþingi majority and a referendum, without an intervening election. Confirmed from the resubmitted bill (Þskj. 5, 142nd session, after the April 2013 election): "Frumvarp þetta er samhljóða frumvarpi því til stjórnarskipunarlaga sem samþykkt var á 141. löggjafarþingi", now with a two-thirds Alþingi threshold and a 40% of electorate floor in the referendum. Not verified: that this became stjórnarskipunarlög 91/2013, and whether the provision was ever used before it lapsed. Not verified: the later history, including the 2016 bill and the 2013 to 2017 commission.
- **Scholarship.** Confirmed (Crossref): Landemore, "Inclusive Constitution-Making: The Icelandic Experiment", Journal of Political Philosophy 23(2) 166–191, doi:10.1111/jopp.12032. Self-description (talk abstracts, not the paper): compares the lay-drafted, crowdsourced draft with two expert drafts and finds it marginally better on religious rights. Her 2014 ConstitutionNet piece calls the experiment "failed" in the title.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** The pipeline Custodian proposes was run end to end: random-sample forum, elected drafters, open comment, advisory referendum with a two-thirds yes. It stopped at the step where an institution had to be bound by the result. The referendum was advisory by law, the Council's legitimacy had been damaged by the annulment before it sat, and the bill ran out of parliamentary time. The handoff gap (docs/book-outline.md, chapter 11) is this case. The annulment also cuts the other way: the capture step itself was contested, so the failure is not only a handoff problem.
- **Open questions.** The tally of the 24 March 2011 vote. Whether the temporary provision was enacted (law number) and why it was never used. What the Council's own members say about the handoff. Whether any Icelandic party has since committed in advance to act on a citizen process, which is the Taiwan Join pattern (still a research gap).
- **Sources.** https://www.althingi.is/altext/stjt/2010.090.html · https://stjornarradid.is/media/forsaetisraduneyti-media/media/stjornarskra/Skyrsla_stjornlaganefndar_fyrra_bindi_Thjodfundur_2010.pdf · https://island.is/s/haestirettur/eldri-akvardanir (Ákvarðanir 2011, 25 January) · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/139/s/1120.pdf · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/139/s/1028.pdf · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/140/s/0831.pdf · https://www.stjornarradid.is/efst-a-baugi/frettir/stok-frett/2012/10/30/Auglysing-um-nidurstodur-thjodaratkvaedagreidslunnar-20.-oktober-2012/ · https://rafhladan.is/bitstream/handle/10802/17948/pub_doc_cLUdNTs.pdf?sequence=1 · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/141/s/0510.pdf · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/141/s/1139.pdf · https://www.althingi.is/altext/pdf/142/s/0005.pdf · https://www.coe.int/en/web/venice-commission/-/cdl-ad-2013-010-e · https://doi.org/10.1111/jopp.12032 · https://constitutionnet.org/news/iceland-five-lessons-failed-experiment-creating-crowdsourced-constitution

## 11. Taiwan: the Join platform's response obligation (提點子, since 2015)
**Status:** Verified (rule text, operator, pass counts from the platform's own open data, three independent evaluations). Checked 2026-10-24.
**Verification tier (primary sources):** institutional-report (Executive Yuan Directions as served by join.gov.tw; National Development Council open data; Bertelsmann Stiftung; NTU thesis). Lin, Wang and Wang 2020: peer-reviewed-journal. PTS and TVBS: news-outlet.

- **The rule.** Confirmed (公共政策網路參與實施要點, Directions for Implementing Online Participation in Public Policy, Executive Yuan order 院授發資字第1041500911號 of 17 July 2015, last revised 11 April 2018 by order 1071500579B; the version served by join.gov.tw on 2026-10-24): the platform is run by the National Development Council (point 2(1)). Anyone with Taiwanese nationality or a residence permit may propose after one-time phone and email verification (point 4). A proposal becomes a case ("成案") when "提議應於六十日內完成五千份附議" (point 7(2): 5,000 endorsements within 60 days). On the day after the threshold is reached the NDC notifies the proposer and the responsible agency, which "應即依第八點規定進行回應" (must respond under point 8). Point 8(3) to (5): the lead agency must draft a concrete response, contact the proposer, may hold a consultation meeting with the proposer present, must publish the response on the platform stating whether the proposal is adopted, partly adopted, under study or rejected and why, and has two months to do so, extendable once by up to two months with the reason posted. Point 9: proposals and endorsements pause for 60 days before a national election. Point 21(1): the NDC may adjust the threshold as the public's familiarity grows. Point 21(2): "參與平臺不刪除任何提議", the platform deletes no proposal; passed, failed, withdrawn and removed proposals all stay searchable.
- **Scope limits.** Confirmed (point 5): only matters within the Executive Yuan and its agencies; excluded are the presidency, other branches, local governments, cross-strait, foreign and defence issues, bills already sent to the legislature, and matters before the courts.
- **Scale and pass rate.** Confirmed from the platform's own open data (data.gov.tw dataset 58036, yearly JSON files from join.gov.tw, downloaded 2026-10-24 and counted): 2020 to 2024, 12,842 proposals submitted, of which 11,125 entered endorsement; 162 reached 5,000 (48, 41, 26, 26, 21 by year), about 1.5% of those endorsed. Method: count of rows with threshold 5,000 and endorsement count at or above it; whether each reached it inside 60 days is not in the data. Consistent with independent counts: 127 of 2,135 endorsed (5.95%) from launch on 10 September 2015 to 31 January 2018 (Lin, Wang and Wang 2020); 202 cases from more than 9,000 proposals by August 2020 (PTS, quoting the NDC); 289 of 13,853 by June 2022 (Bertelsmann Stiftung).
- **Does the obligation bind?** Confirmed (Chen Kun-yi, NTU master's thesis 2019, 156 responded cases, September 2015 to December 2018): "most ministries can't achieve response limit of 60 days"; substantive responses rose over time while speed fell; proposals touching statute or regulation got thinner answers than those touching administrative rules; some agencies answered without consulting anyone outside. Confirmed (Lin, Wang and Wang, 行政暨政策學報 71, December 2020, pp. 1–42): proposals backed by organised interest groups pass the threshold more often; proposals with widely distributed costs pass less often. Confirmed (Bertelsmann Stiftung, September 2022): responses "tend to be thorough and genuine", but there is little deliberation during endorsement and 5,000 "is a low threshold". Confirmed (PTS 2020): the NDC deputy minister says proposals that fail the threshold are also reviewed, and an NDC official gives drunk-driving as a case that took one to two years after the two-month response.
- **Still live.** Confirmed (TVBS World Taiwan, 24 November 2025): a three-day-weekend proposal passed on 7 October 2025 with over 5,700 endorsements and a government response due by 7 December. TVBS states the response deadline as 60 days; the Directions say two months plus one extension.
- **Strongest overlap with Custodian.** This is the only working case found so far of the handoff design in docs/book-outline.md chapter 11: the public sets a question, and an institution is bound to answer it in public within a deadline, but not bound to adopt it. Agenda power without decision power, written into an executive directive rather than statute. Three Custodian echoes in the rule itself: nothing is deleted and failed proposals stay visible (point 21); the threshold is a tunable parameter, not a constant (point 21); the response must state its reasons and is logged on the platform (point 8). The evidence also shows the limits: deadlines are missed, organised groups pass more easily, and 1.5% to 6% of endorsed proposals reach the threshold.
- **Open questions.** Has the Directions text been revised after April 2018, and is the two-month clause still current? What share of responses are "adopted" versus "under study", by year? Why did the pass rate fall from about 6% (2015 to 2018) to about 1.5% (2020 to 2024): more proposals, fewer endorsers, or both? Is the obligation enforceable against an agency that does not answer, or does it rest on the NDC's reporting duty to the Executive Yuan (point 17)? Would Alþingi or the Icelandic government accept a directive of this shape?
